Channel 07 · AS3935 · Radio 500 kHz

Storms, heard before they arrive

A lightning stroke is a radio transmitter a few kilometres long. It fires once, broadcasts across the spectrum, and the crack of it reaches this antenna at the speed of light — minutes before the thunder, and often long before the rain.

The detector does not measure distance so much as estimate it, from the shape and energy of the sferic. It reports one of fourteen bands — the ticks above — so a strike is somewhere in a band, not at a point. Overhead and out of range are flags rather than distances.

Blue hours are complete; the amber bar is the hour still being written, so it climbs as events land. The dashed line is the site's long-term rate — the background chatter of motors and dimmers. A tower rising over that line in the middle of a storm is mostly intra-cloud lightning the chip declined to call strikes.

Readings arrive on the public MQTT broker at wss://live.luisterpost.io, topics luisterpost/lightning/status, luisterpost/lightning/strike and luisterpost/lightning/activity. The sensor is an AMS AS3935 on a tuned 500 kHz loop antenna, on the mast, in a box that holds no metal near the coil.

Why the detector reports its own health. A lightning sensor that has failed and a sky with no lightning in it look exactly the same from outside: zero. So the station publishes whether the chip is answering and whether the antenna is in tune, and this page shows both. Without them a long quiet spell is ambiguous — and this instrument spent three days in August reporting a perfectly peaceful sky while its chip was not on the bus at all.

Interference is a measurement, not noise. The AS3935 flags waveforms that look almost like a stroke but are not — a motor brush, a dimmer, a switching supply. Most installations mask them. This one deliberately does not, because the rate of them is the only direct measure of how electrically hostile the site is, and it is what makes a quiet strike count meaningful. On a bench indoors this sensor logged sixty-two an hour before breakfast; on the mast it reports none for hours at a time. That difference is the whole argument for putting it up there.

What counts as a strike. Most lightning never touches the ground: flashes inside and between clouds outnumber cloud-to-ground strokes several times over, and they are often what you hear as a close rumble with no bolt to be seen. The chip's validation is tuned to the sharp radio signature of a return stroke — the cloud-to-ground kind — and it has no category for "lightning, but not a strike". An intra-cloud flash that fails the check is either dropped in silence or filed under interference, indistinguishable there from a dimmer. So thunder overhead with no new strike on this page is not necessarily a fault; it is the instrument declining to guess. A burst of interference events in the middle of a storm is usually that same story told sideways.

What is published and what is not. The counts, the distance band and the instrument's health go out. The energy figure the chip reports is a bare number on an arbitrary scale — not joules, not comparable between sensors — so it is carried but never dressed up as a physical quantity. Signal strength, uptime and the antenna's tuning capacitor stay on the house broker; they describe the station rather than the sky.

Distance is to the storm front — the leading edge of the active cell — and never to any particular stroke you might have seen. One detector gives a range and no bearing, so nothing here can be triangulated back to a position. The station's own location is published at city precision, as everywhere else.
