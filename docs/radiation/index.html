The background, continuously

A Radiacode RC110 scintillation detector counting gamma photons in a house in Utrecht. Cosmic rays, traces in the building materials, radon washed out of the air by rain — the ordinary radiation everyone lives in, measured every few seconds.

K-40 lock: pending

Every gamma photon the detector absorbs is sorted by its energy. The bumps are fingerprints: potassium-40 at 1461 keV comes from the potassium in concrete, brick and soil — and in bananas. Lead-214 and bismuth-214 are radon daughters, drifting in from the ground. Thallium-208 belongs to the thorium series. Nothing here is unusual; it is what every building quietly contains.

The same spectrum, one row per hour, newest on top. A steady house shows steady bands. When rain washes radon daughters out of the air, the Pb-214 and Bi-214 region darkens for a few hours and then fades — an event the 24-hour sum above averages away entirely.

The bars on the right are the detector's catch-all: everything arriving with more energy than the top of its range, about 2.8 MeV. Nothing in ordinary building materials reaches that high — these are mostly cosmic-ray secondaries, the shower of particles left over when something from space hits the top of the atmosphere. They have no energy value of their own, which is why they cannot appear on the spectrum above.

Live values arrive on the public MQTT broker at wss://live.luisterpost.io, topic luisterpost/radiation/realtime, roughly every three seconds. The three-hour trace is published as one-minute means on luisterpost/radiation/history so a page opened just now still has something to show.

The spectrum is the sum of the last 24 hourly frames, published on luisterpost/radiation/spectrum with its accumulated live time. Channels are grouped in fours for the chart — this detector resolves roughly 70 keV at the potassium line, far wider than one channel, so nothing real is lost. The analyser's final overflow channel is present in the data but not drawn.

Temperature compensation. A scintillator's gain depends on temperature. As the house warms and cools the same photon produces a slightly different pulse height, so it lands in a different channel — the whole energy axis slides back and forth over a day, and the named lines drift off their markers. Nothing about the radiation has changed; only the ruler has.

Potassium-40 is the ruler's fixed mark. It is in every building — concrete, brick, soil — its line sits at a known 1460.8 keV, and it is the strongest identifiable feature in an ordinary background spectrum. So the station measures where that line actually landed and rescales the axis until it sits where it belongs:

The continuum under the peak is estimated by averaging two side bands, 1300–1360 keV below and 1560–1620 keV above, and interpolating linearly between them. That continuum is subtracted across the 1380–1545 keV window, and the centroid of what is left — the counts belonging to the peak itself — is the measured line position. The whole axis is then multiplied by 1460.8 divided by that centroid. A factor of ×1.006 means the detector was reading about 0.6% low.

Two guards. The fit runs on the accumulated sum, never a single hour: one hour holds only a handful of counts at 1461 keV and the centroid would be noise. And it refuses to lock at all below 100 net counts in the peak, or if the centroid lands outside 1400–1530 keV, which would mean the fit had found something that is not potassium. When it declines, the line above the spectrum says pending and the uncorrected axis is used — the station would rather show you a drifting ruler than a confident wrong one.

Dose rate is what the instrument reports, not a measurement of anyone's exposure. A scintillation detector on a shelf indoors is not a personal dosimeter, and short excursions are normal — rain washes radon daughters out of the air and the trace lifts for an hour or two.
