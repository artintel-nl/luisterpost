The background, continuously

A Radiacode RC110 scintillation detector counting gamma photons in a house in Utrecht. Cosmic rays, traces in the building materials, radon washed out of the air by rain — the ordinary radiation everyone lives in, measured every few seconds.

Natural background at ground level is normally somewhere around 0.05–0.15 µSv/h, varying with geology, altitude and weather. This station's own long-run median is 0.082 µSv/h.

For scale: a single chest X-ray is roughly 20 µSv, and an Amsterdam–New York flight around 30–50 µSv in total, because there is far less atmosphere overhead to absorb cosmic rays.

Live values arrive on the public MQTT broker at wss://live.luisterpost.io, topic luisterpost/radiation/realtime, roughly every three seconds. The three-hour trace is published as one-minute means on luisterpost/radiation/history so a page opened just now still has something to show.

Dose rate is what the instrument reports, not a measurement of anyone's exposure. A scintillation detector on a shelf indoors is not a personal dosimeter, and short excursions are normal — rain washes radon daughters out of the air and the trace lifts for an hour or two.
